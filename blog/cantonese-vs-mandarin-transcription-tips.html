Cantonese vs Mandarin transcription: tips for better accuracy

By QuickNotes Team · September 19, 2026 · 5 min read

If you study or work in a Chinese-speaking environment, you have probably wondered why voice-to-text sometimes produces confusing output. Often the reason is simple: the recording was in one language and the tool expected another. Here is how Cantonese and Mandarin differ for transcription, and what you can do at the recording stage to get cleaner results.

Cantonese and Mandarin are different spoken languages

Cantonese and Mandarin share a large writing system, but as spoken languages they differ in pronunciation, tones, vocabulary and grammar. A Cantonese speaker and a Mandarin speaker usually cannot understand each other without learning the other's language. Cantonese is widely spoken in Hong Kong, Macau and Guangdong, while Mandarin (Putonghua) is the standard spoken language in mainland China and Taiwan, where it is often called Guoyu.

For transcription, this means you cannot treat one as a setting variant of the other. A system listening for Mandarin sounds will struggle with Cantonese speech, and the reverse is also true. Choosing the right setting

  1. Match the spoken language, not the writing you want. If the speaker talks in Cantonese, choose Cantonese, even if your course readings are in standard written Chinese.
  2. Pick the main language for mixed recordings. Many lectures switch between Cantonese and English terms. Choose the language that carries most of the speech and expect to check the English words.
  3. Choose the script for Mandarin. QuickNotes offers Mandarin in Simplified and Traditional. Pick the form you prefer to read.
  4. Run a short test. Record one minute, transcribe it, and see how it reads. If it looks wrong, check the language choice first.

Recording tips that improve accuracy in either language

Tones carry meaning in both Cantonese and Mandarin, so clear audio matters. Anything that blurs the sound, such as distance, echo or noise, makes it harder for the system to tell words apart.

Mic distance

Distance is the largest factor. In a quiet room, a phone within roughly an arm's length of the speaker records the voice clearly. Across a big lecture hall, the voice becomes thin and echoey. Sit in the front third if you can, and point the phone's microphone (usually the bottom edge of an iPhone) at the speaker. For interviews, place the phone between you and the other person, closer to whoever is quieter.

Background noise

Noise competes with speech. Turn off fans and music, close doors and windows, and avoid busy cafes. In a classroom, keep the phone away from laptops and rustling paper, and do not put it on a surface that carries vibration from typing. A steady hum is less harmful than sudden noises, but neither helps.

Speaking pace and turn-taking

If you control the session, ask people to speak one at a time and at a natural pace. Overlapping speech is hard for any transcription system, and it is worse when it also mixes tones and languages. Where possible, have speakers say names and figures clearly.

Do a test first

Before a long recording, capture ten seconds, play it back, and check whether the speaker is clearly audible. It takes a moment and can save a whole lecture.

Handling mixed Cantonese and English

In Hong Kong classrooms and offices, it is normal to speak mainly in Cantonese and drop in English course terms, names and acronyms. Transcription can be less reliable on those switches. After you get a transcript, do a quick pass over technical terms and names, and correct them against your slides. It usually takes a few minutes and makes the summary and flashcards built from the transcript more reliable.

What accuracy depends on

  • Audio quality: distance, echo and noise.
  • The speaker: accent, speed and clarity.
  • Vocabulary: everyday speech is easier than jargon and rare names.
  • Language choice: the wrong setting can ruin an otherwise good recording.

Because we cannot promise a particular level of accuracy for every recording, treat any transcript as a draft, and check key information such as dates, figures and names before you rely on it.

A ten-second checklist before you press record

  • Is the language setting going to match the speech?
  • Is the microphone unblocked and close enough?
  • Is the room as quiet as you can make it?
  • Have you done a short test playback?

Getting these four things right costs less than a minute and often matters more than anything you do to the recording afterwards.

A note on permission

If you are recording a lecture, interview or meeting, get consent from the people involved, and check your school or workplace policy. This is general guidance and not legal advice.

The short version: choose the language that is actually spoken, get the microphone close, reduce noise, run a quick test, and review the transcript for mixed-language terms. Those steps usually make the biggest difference.
